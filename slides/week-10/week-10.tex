\begin{frame}{What is an API?}
    An \textbf{API} (Application Programming Interface) is a \textbf{contract}. It specifies what you can request, which parameters you can send, and the format of the response.

    \bigskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Clean \textbf{JSON}, not cluttered HTML
        \item \textbf{Stable endpoints}, not fragile CSS selectors (mostly)
        \item \textbf{Documentation} tells you exactly what exists and how to get it
    \end{itemize}

    \bigskip

    The tradeoff: an API only exposes what its operator \textit{chooses} to. You cannot request a field that the API does not include, exceed the rate limits, or rely on the API forever. 

    \bigskip
    
    The ``post-API age'' from Week 03 should land harder now: many APIs have been restricted, repriced, or shut down --- which is frustrating when you see how powerful they can be!
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Wikipedia, and why now}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}\small
            One of the last big \textbf{open} data sources on the web: a free API, an open license, and public database dumps.

            \medskip

            This openness is under pressure from AI:
            \begin{itemize}\small
                \item \textbf{Crawlers.} Bots made about 35\% of pageviews but at least 65\% of the costliest traffic
                \item \textbf{Readers.} With better bot detection, human pageviews for March--August 2025 came out about 8\% lower than a year before.
                \item \textbf{Writing.} Editors clean up AI-written text, and a 2026 guideline bars using language models to write articles.
            \end{itemize}

        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}

    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{MediaWiki: the software behind Wikipedia}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
            Wikipedia runs on \textbf{MediaWiki}, free and open-source wiki software. The same software runs Wiktionary, Wikidata, Wikimedia Commons, and thousands of other wikis.
            \begin{itemize}\small
                \item Every page you read is drawn from MediaWiki's database: the article's wikitext, its history, its links.
                \item The \textbf{Action API} serves that same database as data. Wikipedia's own tools use it, and so do the bots that patrol edits and researchers counting what's there.
                \item Every MediaWiki site has one, at \texttt{api.php}: learn it on Wikipedia, and it works on the others.
            \end{itemize}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
            \begin{block}{A basic request}
                \footnotesize
                \texttt{https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php}\\[0.3em]
                \begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
                    \texttt{?action=query} & what to do \\
                    \texttt{\&titles=University\_of\_Colorado\_Boulder} & which page \\
                    \texttt{\&prop=info} & what about it \\
                    \texttt{\&format=json} & in what form
                \end{tabular}
            \end{block}
            \small The ``API call'' boxes on the tour that follows show only what changes: the \texttt{prop} or \texttt{list} after \texttt{action=query}.

            \bigskip
            
            Documentation: \href{https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/API:Action_API}{mediawiki.org/wiki/API:Action\_API}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Step 5: your second move}

            \medskip

        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}

        # Keep this page's edits

        # Your second move: add the "continue" pairs

\begin{frame}{Key takeaways}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item An API is a \textbf{documented contract}: ask in its form, and it answers in data.
        \item \textbf{Ask small first}: three edits, three days, three categories. Then the whole thing.

\begin{frame}{A tour: what sits behind every article}
    Like most users, you have probably never clicked any deeper into Wikipedia than an article. But Wikipedia keeps many important records publicly available:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item who changed what, and when --- the \textbf{revision history}
        \item what editors argued about --- the \textbf{talk page}
        \item how many people read it --- \textbf{pageviews}
        \item what it links to, what links to it, and the same topic in other \textbf{languages}
        \item its \textbf{references}, \textbf{categories}, and \textbf{templates}
        \item the editors themselves --- \textbf{user pages} and \textbf{contributions}
    \end{itemize}
    \bigskip
    Each one has an \textbf{API endpoint}. Anything you can see in the interface, you can count.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From the interface to the API}
    \centering\small
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \toprule
        \textbf{What you see} & \textbf{Where} & \textbf{API request} \\
        \midrule
        The article's basic facts & the article & \texttt{prop=info} \\
        Wikilinks out & the article's text & \texttt{prop=links} \\
        What links here & Tools $\rightarrow$ What links here & \texttt{list=backlinks} \\
        Templates & the infobox and other boxes & \texttt{prop=templates} \\
        Categories & the foot of the article & \texttt{prop=categories} \\
        References' links & References & \texttt{prop=extlinks} \\
        Other languages & the languages button & \texttt{prop=langlinks} \\
        Talk page & the Talk tab & any of these, for \texttt{titles=Talk:\ldots} \\
        Revision history & View history & \texttt{prop=revisions} \\
        Pageviews & Page information & REST \texttt{/metrics/pageviews/\ldots} \\
        User contributions & Special:Contributions & \texttt{list=usercontribs} \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

    \medskip
    \raggedright Every Action API request here starts\\ \texttt{https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php?action=query\&format=json}
\end{frame}
